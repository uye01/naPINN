## Summary

The paper presents a methodology for learning how much confidence to place in examples under an inverse physics-informed neural network when data is noisy, corrupted, or comes from faulty sensors. The technique called “naPINN” learns residual distributions to assign a cost to measurements. The technique reduces field error under high levels of noise. Through an ablation study, the study discerns the contribution of both the adaptive module (trainable gate) and the learned residual distribution. 

Overall, the paper is well written and well organized. The proposed technique is interesting and proved to be beneficial in the experimental results. The ablation study shows the value of the involved components (learned density, gate). A formal interpretation of the objective components clarifies the overall architecture. The novelty lies in adding learned residual distributions to handle noise more effectively. This is not a departure from PINNs in general, but a solid contribution to their current mechanism. 

General comments: 

•	It is known that density estimation is harder than building predictive models, and the error in assuming the wrong distribution can have a large impact on the proposed method. How certain is the quality of the probability density estimation? Can we test the quality of the density-estimation method? 

•	Page 4 describes the rejection cost as an expectation over (1-gi), where gi is the reliability weight. But that does not include the residual ri. My understanding is that this penalizes a certain number of rejections. I envision a scenario where rejecting “many” examples with relatively low-to-medium residuals can benefit more than rejecting a single one with a large residual. In this case, wouldn’t it be better to reject the one with the highest residual, even if it comes at the price of a higher total loss? In that case, the goal might not be to minimize total raw loss (in out-of-sample data) but the loss on test examples where outliers are also removed. 

•	The difference in performance between the method having the learned density and gate active is dramatically bigger than when the gate is fixed. A deeper analysis may reveal not just the importance of the trained gate but also some interactions with other components and loss functions. The same is true for the rejection cost. These results may be worth exploring in more depth. 


Page 3, Bottom: the term EBM is used but not defined until later. 
Page 7, bottom: the paper states: “…we report the widening as an association.” This is unclear.

The paper needs deeper analysis to understand how the design components interact.
